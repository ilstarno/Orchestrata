Capacity and constraints by sector

Modeling checklist. These questions do not describe supported features.

Container stowage and vessel capacity

Decisions

  • Compare loading sequences and compatible slots across vessels and ports before proposing a stowage plan.
  • Keep containers with missing or inconsistent verified mass out of the ready-to-load plan.
  • Estimate rehandles and equipment time when berth arrival or discharge order changes.

Capacity to measure

  • Count 20- and 40-foot slot positions separately and record paired-slot conflicts. Total TEU does not establish physical fit.
  • Track metric tonnes per stack, hatch or deck zone; use vessel-specific structural and stability limits, not aggregate deadweight alone.
  • Count compatible powered reefer positions and simultaneous crane moves per hour. Include outages and shore power limits.

Constraints

  • Where SOLAS verified gross mass requirements apply, use the signed verified mass in the stowage process; a booking weight is not a substitute. Verification is a condition of loading, not authority to load.
  • Use vessel-approved Cargo Securing Manual limits and applicable cargo separation rules. A general IMO reference does not supply clearance for a specific ship or commodity.
  • Keep port discharge order, permissible hatch passage, and terminal crane restrictions separate from fleet preferences such as minimizing rehandles.

What can go wrong

  • An open slot has insufficient stack mass margin or the wrong container length.
  • An available reefer box position has no usable power outlet.
  • A revised port sequence traps containers behind later-discharge cargo or invalidates planned weight distribution.

Evidence to collect

  • Ship-approved bay plan with slot compatibility and blocked positions, mass ledger with verified gross mass and timestamps, and equipment windows.
  • Vessel-specific loading and stability calculation signed off through the vessel's approved procedures; a planner replay alone is not proof.

Grocery replenishment and warehouse slots

Decisions

  • Place each lot in a usable slot and allocate replenishment windows without blocking aisles or picking faces.
  • Reserve temperature-controlled space and handling equipment as stock and trucks arrive in waves.
  • Compare a promotion against a normal day using peak-hour labor and dock demand, not just total distance.

Capacity to measure

  • Pallet positions by dimensions and load rating in kg; cubic metres and case facings do not establish weight capacity.
  • Available powered lift trucks and trained operator-minutes; clearance in metres at the equipment's required turning envelope.
  • Dock door-minutes, concurrent staging pallet positions and chilled or frozen positions by approved temperature band in °C.

Constraints

  • Apply local workplace rules on cleared aisles and stable storage. OSHA 29 CFR 1910.176 is a US baseline where it applies, not a global aisle width or forklift load rating.
  • Keep site-required lot rotation, segregation, shelf rules, spill zones and promotional displays separate from regulation.
  • For US transport covered by FDA sanitary transportation requirements, match the food safety specification and agreed responsibilities; transport rules alone do not define warehouse shelf life.

What can go wrong

  • The bin is empty but the loaded pallet cannot be placed safely by available equipment.
  • Promotion volume fills staging before the dock window opens, blocking the receiving path.
  • A chilled lot physically fits in an ambient bay, but the approved handling conditions cannot be met.

Evidence to collect

  • Rack and equipment rating records, bay dimensions, shift-qualified operators and scanned lot and expiry dates.
  • Distinct capacity and queue measurements for receiving, staging, storage and replenishment, with agreed temperature handling evidence.

Fleet duty cycles and depot charging

Decisions

  • Assign routes to vehicles, then schedule charge slots against return times, departure deadlines and minimum reserve.
  • Compare charge sequencing versus added connectors or a service upgrade before attributing shortages to vehicle count.
  • Replan when a charger faults, a route runs late or consumption deviates from the duty-cycle estimate.

Capacity to measure

  • Vehicle energy in usable kWh and percent state of charge; connector output in kW and station occupancy in minutes.
  • Shared electrical service limits in kW or kVA as specified by the site engineer and utility, with building load in the same time intervals.
  • Route payload in kg and m³, driver and vehicle minutes, and depot parking positions. Include energy for auxiliary refrigeration or climate load.

Constraints

  • Treat connector, vehicle acceptance limits, site electrical capacity and approved engineering settings as hard input boundaries; US DOE fleet guidance does not authorize electrical work.
  • Model employer reserve and priority policy separately from legal vehicle load, road access and driver rules. Confirm jurisdiction, journey and vehicle applicability before evaluating.
  • Do not assume that advertised charger power is sustained or that sessions can overlap on one connector; model derating and charge curves.

What can go wrong

  • Several vehicles start at once and exceed the site headroom even though each connector is within its rating.
  • Available energy covers mean route demand but misses reserve after cold weather or credible route delay.
  • The vehicle finishes charging, but the connector only appears compatible in the inventory and is not approved for that model.

Evidence to collect

  • Route and energy telemetry, asset-to-connector compatibility matrix, session event logs, and approved utility/site headroom by interval.
  • Replay a charger outage and late depot return without letting an undercharged vehicle appear dispatchable.

Airport gates, arrivals and ground services

Decisions

  • Assign contact gates and remote stands by aircraft and handling readiness, preserving space for late arrivals.
  • Compare a nominal gate assignment with the resulting baggage, passenger and ground-team workload.
  • Evaluate multi-stand closures or an arrival wave while showing which demand needs manual handling.

Capacity to measure

  • Gate or stand occupancy in aircraft-minutes, apron frontage and clearances in metres, and equipment compatibility by aircraft family.
  • Passenger-processing capacity per 15 minutes by staffed checkpoint, corridor, bus, and eligible arrivals stream; a gate count is not passenger capacity.
  • Qualified ground-team and equipment minutes in the same location and time window, including transfer and reset time.

Constraints

  • An empty stand still needs airport-approved aircraft and adjacent-stand compatibility plus applicable airport conditions. FAA terminal planning guidance supports design work; it is not airport certification or approval.
  • Use local airport and airline rules for international arrivals handling, baggage reclaim, stand closures and service equipment. Carry these as separately versioned site inputs.
  • Protect mandatory buffers and handling dependencies. Only then compare preferences such as contact-gate use, walk distances and towing effort.

What can go wrong

  • Large aircraft can fit each stand individually but cannot occupy neighboring stands at the same time.
  • Moving a flight to a remote stand frees a gate but saturates the coach queue or ground-team schedule.
  • Gate assignment is feasible while baggage or eligible passenger processing is over capacity during the same arrival burst.

Evidence to collect

  • Airport-approved stand and adjacency matrix, terminal and baggage processing rates, ground equipment roster and staff qualifications.
  • Stale closure and adjacency data must hold a recommendation; replay the peak arrival bank and verify no shared resource is double booked.

Commercial rental fleets and workshop capacity

Decisions

  • Match contract-compatible vehicles to pickup windows, including return uncertainty and one-way transfers.
  • Schedule inspection, cleaning, maintenance and recall holds before any next handover.
  • Rebalance approved substitutes between branches without treating unavailable workshop resources as capacity.

Capacity to measure

  • Available vehicle-hours by branch, class and equipment; distinguish physically present assets from release-ready assets.
  • Workshop bay-hours by compatible lift rating and technician-hours by required skill, plus waiting parts and wash or preparation capacity.
  • Transfer driver and vehicle minutes, destination parking slots and booking slack after expected return.

Constraints

  • Separate operator roadworthiness obligations from local contract promises and upgrade/substitute rules. DVSA guidance cited here is for commercial goods or passenger operators, not a universal rental law.
  • Use the applicable inspection and recall records; a calendar flag alone is not a roadworthy-release decision.
  • Allow an out-of-service asset to be moved for permitted servicing only if specifically authorized. Do not treat that movement as rental availability.

What can go wrong

  • An asset is assigned as soon as it returns but needs inspection, repairs or a recall remedy.
  • A substitution satisfies inventory count but fails class, capacity or contract compatibility.
  • A workshop has free bays but lacks a qualified technician or a required part, stranding several bookings.

Evidence to collect

  • Asset state history, booking and substitution contract, parts plan, and technician/bay compatibility.
  • Signed first-use or equivalent evidence where it applies, recall and rectification trace, and a separate handover-release event.
UK DVSA: Guide to maintaining roadworthiness for commercial goods and passenger vehiclesUK DVSA: Maintenance investigation guidance and evidence
Outpatient room and support-team coordination

Decisions

  • Prepare rooms and support staff for appointments already approved by the service.
  • Reassign cleaning, reception coverage, and equipment preparation after a cancellation.

Capacity to measure

  • Ready room-minutes by room class and site, after setup and turnover.
  • Reception and support staff-minutes by shift; equipment units ready in each interval.
  • Booked slots, attended slots, and late cancellations as separate counts.

Constraints

  • Service-owned appointment order and session limits remain fixed.
  • Preparation and turnover must fit resource windows; assigned equipment cannot overlap.
  • Respect the approved room setup without storing the reason for any adjustment.

What can go wrong

  • Treating appointment bookings as attended work conceals wasted slots.
  • A room exists on paper but cannot open because no support worker or equipment is available.
  • Rebooking consumes a later slot and can move backlog instead of eliminating it.

Evidence to collect

  • Replay anonymized cancellations and turnover durations using aggregate arrival and attendance records.
  • Compare opening delays, unused ready room-minutes, held tasks, and gaps by location against the current operating schedule.

School timetabling and specialist room allocation

Decisions

  • Assign groups to class periods, qualified staff, and suitable rooms.
  • Rebuild a day after a closure without unnecessarily moving unaffected classes.

Capacity to measure

  • Seats per usable room by period; specialist lab or workshop stations are a separate limit.
  • Teacher periods and room-periods with setup/cleanup time deducted.
  • Whole-school net capacity is a planning reference, not a per-class room authorization.

Constraints

  • No simultaneous double booking of the room, group, or supervising staff.
  • Use locally verified room designation, accessibility, safety capacity, and staff requirements.
  • Preserve locked periods and allow real travel time between buildings.

What can go wrong

  • Enough total seats but no qualifying lab permits the lesson.
  • Two rooms appear free while the only approved supervisor is elsewhere.
  • A single room outage forces avoidable changes across the entire day.

Evidence to collect

  • Verify each assignment against the room inventory, timetable, and approved staff coverage.
  • Replay a room outage and compare unserved lessons, moved periods, and cross-building travel.

Multi-location shifts and shared relief teams

Decisions

  • Fill shifts across stores and departments using an approved relief pool.
  • Choose whether to fill a staffing gap with local cover, a transfer, or a human escalation.

Capacity to measure

  • Qualified person-hours per site, role, and time band.
  • Transfer travel-minutes, net duty time after breaks, and minimum concurrent role coverage.
  • Relief staff capacity counted once across all participating departments.

Constraints

  • Only approved staff and site-specific competencies count towards coverage.
  • Keep break, leave, availability and site-opening rules distinct; use the validated local work-time profile.
  • Charge transfer time and preserve agreed schedules where feasible.

What can go wrong

  • Two departments use the same floating worker at the same time.
  • Coverage appears adequate at daily granularity but drops below minimum for a key interval.
  • A relief transfer removes coverage at the sending site.

Evidence to collect

  • Compare roster-to-actual coverage, missed intervals, reassignment count and paid travel.
  • Independently validate site permissions, approved absence windows, and constraints before release.

Contact center capacity by queue, skill and interval

Decisions

  • Allocate net staff capacity to voice, chat and deferred tickets.
  • Move coaching or back-office work when wait times rise, without overloading another queue.

Capacity to measure

  • Forecast offered contacts per 15 or 30 minutes and handling seconds including follow-up.
  • Net staffed agent-minutes after unavailable time, with channel-specific concurrency.
  • Deferred backlog count and age distribution tracked separately from live-queue service targets.

Constraints

  • Only authorized skills, languages, and business units are substitutable.
  • Keep concurrent chat limits separate from single-call voice occupancy.
  • Observe coverage during breaks and preapproved protected time.

What can go wrong

  • Good daily coverage hides short queue peaks and missed targets.
  • One multilingual agent is counted at full capacity in two queues.
  • A voice-only objective pushes deferred work beyond its response target.

Evidence to collect

  • Replay actual aggregate arrivals, staffing and handle times by channel and interval.
  • Compare queue wait distribution, target attainment, abandonment, oldest backlog and transfer load; no customer payloads needed.

Transactional printing, insertion and handoff

Decisions

  • Group compatible work around pickup cutoffs; reserve a rerun lane for damaged pieces.
  • Hold suspected duplicate or mismatched pieces before dispatch.

Capacity to measure

  • Press impressions per hour by stock and duplex mode; inserter pieces per hour by envelope and insert profile.
  • Setup and changeover minutes, reprint setup, check-station throughput and dispatch cutoff time.
  • Track usable stock by media lot and production stages, not only gross page count.

Constraints

  • Check press/finisher compatibility and material availability before batching.
  • Keep a single verified release per piece identifier; preserve holds and reconciliation evidence.
  • Treat print-floor release and carrier acceptance as separate checks.

What can go wrong

  • Optimizing print runs alone moves the queue to insertion.
  • A fast rerun releases the original and replacement without deduplication.
  • A batch meets press schedule but misses carrier handoff.

Evidence to collect

  • Replay historical job tickets and independent scan logs with duplicate, mismatch and missing events.
  • Reconcile verified pieces, holds and carrier documentation; compare waste, idle time and cutoff attainment.

Facilities maintenance and work-order backlog

Decisions

  • Slot preventive and corrective work around staffed coverage and tenant access.
  • Replan the low-priority backlog when an urgent fault consumes a crew or a part is delayed.

Capacity to measure

  • Qualified technician-hours, shared lift/tool-hours, verified spare units and access-window minutes.
  • Separate travel and staging from hands-on work; keep callout capacity reserved.

Constraints

  • Access and owner-approved safety prerequisites are gates, not scores.
  • Reserve a crew for multi-person jobs; count shared tools once.
  • Treat expected-part arrival and onsite confirmation differently; lock agreed facility windows as required.

What can go wrong

  • A visit is scheduled while the part, access permission, or owner signoff is missing.
  • Shared equipment ties up two otherwise workable crews.
  • Reactive work repeatedly displaces preventive work without recording the maintenance debt.

Evidence to collect

  • Verify work-order readiness and closure with maintenance records, including reopened jobs.
  • Compare first-visit completion, overdue preventive items, return trips and unresolved urgent demand.

DNS and load balancing

Decisions

  • Choose the regions new DNS lookups can use.
  • Allocate incoming traffic to eligible services or keep excess demand unserved.

Capacity to measure

  • Requests or connections per second, active connections, and reserve capacity by region.
  • Independent failure domains with enough healthy capacity to absorb a shift.

Constraints

  • Approved regions, probe freshness, required coverage after failure, and a reviewed fallback.
  • DNS cache lifetime, sticky sessions, and existing connections limit how quickly traffic moves.

What can go wrong

  • Stale health appears current; all targets fail and a provider returns an unhealthy destination; answer weights do not match request shares.

Evidence to collect

  • Recorded probe and load snapshots with units and timestamps; test missing health, all-unhealthy pools, cache lag, and demand over capacity.

Platform services and shared capacity

Decisions

  • Set admit, queue, or defer limits per kind of work.
  • Keep headroom for critical work when a shared dependency slows down.

Capacity to measure

  • Downstream concurrent work, queue service rate, and spare capacity before the latency target is lost.
  • Retry volume and the amount of work one customer or service can consume.

Constraints

  • Priority policy, maximum queue age, fairness across work streams, bounded retries and backoff.

Decisions

  • Place workloads on nodes and zones that can run them.

Capacity to measure

  • Schedulable CPU and memory, usable accelerators, compatible nodes, and placement room in each zone.

Constraints

Multi-region recovery

Decisions

  • Choose which region can take traffic and when a return is safe.
  • Prioritize services and data restoration when the standby cannot handle the full load.

Capacity to measure

  • Failover compute, storage write throughput, replication bandwidth, and dependencies that have warmed up.
  • Recovery time and tolerated data loss are separate targets.

Constraints

  • Data placement policy, observed replication lag, allowed write leadership and service dependency order.
  • Require evidence that standby and critical dependencies are ready, not just a reachable endpoint.

What can go wrong

  • A standby can receive traffic but lacks key dependencies; separate sites accept conflicting writes; failback starts before the replica catches up.

Evidence to collect

  • Recovery objectives approved per service, recent measured lag and restoration tests, and a versioned policy for switching writes.
  • Replay regional loss and delayed replication; report both time-to-restore and measurable recovery point.

Storage and network headroom

Decisions

  • Choose where volumes can be placed alongside workloads.
  • Schedule data copies and backups without crowding out customer traffic.

Capacity to measure

  • Free capacity by storage class and zone, write throughput, input/output operations per second, and cross-zone link bandwidth.
  • Reserved bandwidth and time for periodic backups or replication.

Constraints

  • Access mode, volume topology, data locality, per-link capacity, and approved backup windows.

  • Free storage exists only in the wrong zone; a failed link overloads the remaining path; backup I/O displaces application writes.

Construction equipment and lift-window planning

Decisions

  • Reserve crews and equipment around permit, delivery and weather windows.
  • Hold a proposed window when the required qualified review is missing.

Capacity to measure

  • Equipment hours, setup areas, spotters and supervisors, verified equipment-specific ratings.

Constraints

  • Do not infer safe rated capacity from a nominal crane tonnage. A competent person addresses effects of wind, ice or snow under applicable OSHA rules.
